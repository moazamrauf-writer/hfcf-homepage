Talking to a Child Who Has Lost a Parent

There is no perfect script. A few simple habits can help a grieving child feel heard and less alone.

Section 1 · Intro
Section 2 · Article
Sample content9 November 2026 · HFC Foundation Team

Adults often worry about saying the wrong thing to a child who is grieving. In truth, children usually remember how safe they felt more than the exact words they were told.

Be Honest, in Simple Words

Children can tell when they are not being told the truth, and silence can make them imagine something worse. Use short, clear words that fit her age. If you do not know the answer to a question, it is fine to say so.

Let Her Lead

Some children want to talk straight away. Others need weeks, or months, before they say anything. Do not push. Make it clear that she can ask or talk whenever she is ready, and that she will not be in trouble for whatever she feels.

Expect Grief to Look Different

Grief in children does not always look like sadness. It can show up as anger, clinging, trouble sleeping, stomach aches or a sudden drop in schoolwork. These are common ways of coping, and they usually ease when a child feels safe and cared for.

Keep the Same Faces Around Her

Steady, familiar caretakers matter a great deal. They are the people she can test, lean on and come back to. When a child has been through a loss, consistency is one of the kindest things we can offer.

When to Ask for Professional Help

If a child seems stuck in her distress for a long time, stops eating or sleeping, or talks about harming herself, please speak to a child psychologist or a doctor. Asking for help is a sign of care, not of failure.
